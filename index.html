Edge of Sync

A computational model of collective transitions in a dancing group

This project models how a dancing group moves into and out of synchrony in a closed loop, where the dancers’ own movement generates the music they hear. Each dancer is an oscillator with a tempo of their own, moving across a shared floor, and the strength of their coupling learns from their shared history. The model asks when and how the collective shifts between states of synchrony, from either direction, how near to a transition the group is at any moment, and whether groups hold themselves at that edge. It is the generative counterpart to an experimental study that will track movement, sound and heart rate, and ask whether these transitions carry consequences for altered states of consciousness and wellbeing.

The model

Each skeleton is one dancer

Dancers move, keep their own tempo, listen to the music their group makes, and learn whom to move with.

The group writes the music as it dances. Apart, each dancer sounds their own note, out of tune with the rest. As synchrony builds, a song forms around the shared beat: a pulse, then bass and moving chords, then a melody that settles into a repeating hook. The song lingers for a few seconds when synchrony drops. With the loop closed, every layer pulls the dancers toward the beat, the low percussion most of all.

What happened

    Description

    What you see. Each figure is a dancer, drawn as the 17-joint skeleton that pose extraction produces (COCO keypoints: nose, eyes, ears, shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees and ankles). Its limbs move through a simple dance on its own movement cycle: a dip on the beat, alternating steps and swinging arms, each dancer in a style of their own. Its color is a heat map of its local synchrony: how closely its phase matches the dancers nearby, weighted by distance. Pale slate means it keeps its own beat; amber to deep red means it moves as one with its neighbors. A small dark marker above the head marks a leader: a dancer slightly ahead of synchronized neighbors, who keep catching up to it. When colors match, dancers are in step. Teal threads join pairs whose learned coupling is strong. The ring around the floor glows with the same heat colors: each part of it shows the synchrony of the dancers nearest to it, and the whole ring warms when the group moves as one. The floor itself takes a faint tint of the collective state: apart, clusters, together or a phase wave.

    The phase portrait. The dish above the floor is the group’s phase space. The ball is the order parameter: its distance from the center is the phase synchrony R (center apart, rim together), and its angle is the group’s mean phase relative to the shared tempo, so a steady group sits still and a drifting one circles. The dish’s height is the landscape from the last minute: troughs are wells the group keeps returning to, ridges are the barriers between them. The trail shows the last few seconds of the trajectory.

    The engine. Movement follows the swarmalator model: dancers are drawn toward one another, more so toward those in phase, while keeping personal space; with self-propulsion they also travel in a direction, as directional swarmalators do. Couzin’s zones of repulsion, alignment and attraction are an alternative movement rule. Phases couple as in the Kuramoto model, weighted by proximity, around each dancer’s own natural tempo. With adaptive coupling on, each pair’s coupling strengthens when they move in phase and weakens when they do not, so the group learns its own coordination; a plasticity lag makes that learning non-reciprocal, which can leave the group restlessly switching between states (metastability).

    The closed loop. The group’s movement writes the music. A song oscillator follows the group’s mean phase, the way a band follows its dancers, and its strength builds as synchrony rises and lingers for a few seconds after it falls: the song’s memory. That strength brings in the music in layers: a low percussive beat first, then harmony that moves from chord to chord, then a melody that settles into a repeating hook the longer the group stays together. With the loop closed, dancers hear the song a moment late and lean toward its beat, and each layer adds to that pull: the low beat counts most (60%), moving harmony next (25%) and a settled hook least (15%), following evidence that low-frequency percussive sound drives movement most strongly. Because the song grows from synchrony and in turn sustains it, the loop can make two states stable at once, together and apart, which the landscape shows as two wells and the sweep shows as hysteresis. A longer song memory holds the group together through brief lapses.

    Who is dancing. Strangers start with varied tempos and weak ties that they must learn as they dance, so they usually find a shared beat only after a minute or more. Professional dancers keep precise tempos, move with little noise and are tuned to the music, so they lock together quickly. Friends arrive in small groups with similar tempos and lasting ties inside each group and weak ties to everyone else, so synchrony forms within friend groups first and usually spreads, though the groups sometimes stay in cliques.

    The fog machine. Fog cuts what dancers can see but not what they hear. Inside a puff, each dancer’s coupling to the others weakens in proportion to the fog around both of them, for phase and for being drawn toward those in phase, while the pull of the music is unchanged. The puff spreads and thins over about ten seconds. Whether the group holds together through the fog shows how much its synchrony depends on seeing one another and how much the music carries it.

    Reading transitions. A transition is the group crossing from one well to the other. Watch the early-warning panel as the group nears the edge, click the floor to release fog or scramble the rhythm of nearby dancers and see how long the group takes to recover, and run the sweep to see whether it tips at different points coming from each direction.
